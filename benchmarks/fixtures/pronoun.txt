The Pacific Reef Resilience Program began in 1991 as a partnership between three island nations and a single underfunded marine station on a leased atoll. In its first decade the program mapped over two hundred reef systems, cataloguing coral cover, fish biomass, and water chemistry at a level of detail that no regional survey had attempted before. The field teams worked from small boats in difficult conditions, and the data they brought back reshaped how regional governments thought about marine protected areas. Funding followed the results, slowly at first and then in larger grants, which allowed the program to build permanent monitoring stations on eleven atolls and to train local technicians to run them. By the time the program celebrated its twentieth anniversary it had produced the longest continuous reef health record in the southern hemisphere, and much of the credit for that record belongs to the program's longest-serving director, Dr. Amara Whitfield, who joined as a field assistant and spent three decades studying coral reefs across the South Pacific.

She argued that the most damaging assumption in the field was that reefs recover on their own. Her data showed recovery only happens when local fishing pressure drops at the same time.

The institute she founded now trains researchers from twelve countries. It operates on a shoestring budget, which is something that has been a source of frustration for the staff in order to maintain operations.
